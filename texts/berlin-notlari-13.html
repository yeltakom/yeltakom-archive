Berlin Notes #13

Berlin Notları #13, Turkish

Today is 6 April 2019, and I am continuing the Berlin Notes that I have not written for a long time.

On an unexpected spring day in Berlin, I am preparing a banner for the first time in a long while. Meanwhile I am messaging with friends and waiting for Elif at home. I do not have much experience with German banners. I have a banner I prepared the day before that says "Architektur für Alle" on one side and "Herkes İçin Mimarlık" on the other, but I am looking for an alternative. The #Fridays4Future action of the previous week, which Greta also joined, is an inspiration; borrowing from "There is no Planet B" I write "Es gibt keinen Berlin B" [There is no Berlin B], "Wohnen ist Menschenrecht" [Dwelling is a human right] and "Das Problem heisst Kapitalismus" [The problem is called capitalism]. Of course, falling victim to the cruelty of the Artikel (the article), I end my banner preparation with "keinen" instead of writing "kein".

As we head to the metro with banners in our hands, it feels as if everyone around us is going to Alexanderplatz. Because one of the biggest things this city has in common is that almost 85 percent of its residents are tenants. I have written before about how finding a rental home in Berlin has become harder and how the city is getting more and more expensive. There is a serious housing problem in the city; big investment and construction companies, rather than building new structures, renovate housing that needs refurbishment and rent it out again at a high price. Another of the most important reasons for high rents is the migration of qualified labour; since the new qualified migrants can afford the high rents, old tenants, especially in central neighbourhoods, can be displaced.

The main slogan of the march, "Together Against Crazy Rents, Against Displacement!", called on all tenants living in Berlin to act for a more equal city. This demand for the right to the city wanted a city where everyone can breathe, regardless of where we come from, the language we speak, our gender, our age and our health. Like similar cities in the world, Berlin is now one of the places where real estate, or in other words the city itself, is seen as a "business model". The city, which for a long time was the apple of the eye of newly founded tech companies because of its reputation for being cheap, is now dealing with what that has brought. I see this as consuming a city, even scraping its bottom. The dreams that seemed beautiful to everyone at first, as they take flesh and bone, enlarge the invisible problems alongside them. Yet is it not possible for city dwellers to live through this process without being caught in the whirlpool of gentrification by their own hands? It may be possible, but on the other hand it is a whirlpool that is loved. It is important to think about this question; I am sure our alternative models will come out of here.

They say there were about 35 thousand people at the protest that day. I had not felt this good at a march for a long time; it was like a festival. Karl-Marx-Allee, the monumental avenue the GDR used for its national celebrations, had become a place for the right to the city with its colourful protesters. It is worth reminding, in every possible field, that the right to the city is a shared concern beyond a matter of individual freedom. Although this shared concern is disputed by some, it is quite important to accept the fact that the city belongs to those who live in it, that is, to us, and to start and be part of common discussions about our living spaces. After all, when it comes to public space, although the "idea that space is open to everyone", long since on everyone's lips, is sometimes found outdated, it is still a fact that this openness holds many potentials. What we call public space is in essence nothing but a constructed concept, but common life, our commons, what we share, make up parts of the right to the city. Especially today, not only public space but our rents, our data, all are part of our common rights. The discourse built around public space often cannot go beyond repeating the language of the powerful. The private, the opposite of the public, is also guaranteed by the state as property, just as the public is "guaranteed" by the state. This approach sometimes sharpens the difference between public and private space and sometimes blurs it. It can easily be said that the state creates a spatial domination over society with these decision mechanisms.

One of the headlines that caught my attention in the newspapers after the protest in Berlin questioned whether this was a social movement or just a march. These protests took place simultaneously not only in Berlin but also in other cities such as Barcelona, Cologne, Dortmund, Dresden, Freiburg, Jena, Leipzig, Lisbon, Munich, Paris, Potsdam and Stuttgart. While in past months, asking "Has the End of the Internet as We Know It Come?", this month I can say: have we come to the end of the forms of housing as we know them? A housing model that is not renewed but re-established may perhaps be a starting point for cities, if these massifying movements are going to start a transformation. This transformation, as David Harvey says referring to Marx, will transform ourselves too.

This massification brings the user, the city dweller, into the process at every layer. The transformation that comes with massification will not be one-sided; first we will think we are transforming the world, then we will realise that we are actually transforming ourselves.

Towards the end of the protest we return to our neighbourhood. While trying to reach our home through roads closed by the German police, with the banners in our hands we see our fellow protesters, our neighbours. A strange feeling of belonging; an interesting sensation that feels good and yet avoids unnecessary intimacy.

Let me end the text by saying, wherever we are, "the house is rented but the neighbourhood is ours". If you want to listen to Turkey's first tenants' movement, you can listen to the Metropolitika programme with Yaşar Adanalı, and look at the work of the Düzce Umut Atölyesi and Kiracıyız Biz.
