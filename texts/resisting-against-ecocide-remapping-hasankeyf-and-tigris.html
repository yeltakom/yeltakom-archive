Resisting Against Ecocide: Remapping Hasankeyf and Tigris

2024 · Ecologies of Making Worlds conference · No. 2411

Original ↗

Original, English

"Resisting Against Ecocide: Remapping Hasankeyf and Tigris" delves into the development of decolonial visual representations to confront the dispossession inflicted by large-scale extractivist projects. The research examines how landscapes, settlements, and communities in southeastern Turkey, specifically Hasankeyf and the Tigris River region, resist the ecological and cultural erasure imposed by state-driven infrastructure projects such as the Ilisu Dam. This exploration combines critical cartography, participatory documentation, and interdisciplinary analysis to highlight the region's resilience and craft alternative dispossession mappings.

The Ilisu Dam, part of Turkey's Southeastern Anatolia Project (GAP), is a stark example of how state-led projects can deeply impact the environment and cultural heritage. Conceived in the 1950s and accelerated in the 1970s, the dam submerged Hasankeyf, a site with 12,000 years of continuous human settlement. This loss is not just of a geographical space but of an invaluable archive of human history, which included ancient caves, mosques, and historically significant structures. The flooding severed the deep connections that communities had with their land, disrupting cultural continuity and displacing more than 78,000 people.

Documenting and remapping this transformed landscape involves adopting methodologies beyond traditional cartography. The process integrates visual, auditory, and participatory elements to create a multi-sensory archive. This approach aims to produce a counter-narrative to the official state representations that frame such projects as purely progressive. By doing so, the project illuminates the ecological and human costs of extractivist ventures and underscores the resilience of displaced communities.

The notion of alternative mapping is pivotal here. Traditional maps often serve as tools of state power, reinforcing hegemonic narratives that align with economic and infrastructural goals. However, decolonial mapping seeks to disrupt this power dynamic by integrating local knowledge, community experiences, and the voices of those most affected. Through interviews, fieldwork, and participatory mapping, new stories emerge that challenge the singular narrative of development.

Elizabeth Povinelli's concept of "geontopower" offers a lens through which to understand the intersection of governance, economic strategies, and cultural persistence. In the context of Hasankeyf, counter-archiving becomes an act of resistance, preserving not only the tangible evidence of what was lost but also the intangible sense of identity and heritage. This archive, rooted in lived experience, allows alternative narratives to come to light, challenging the state's portrayal of progress.

The visual presentation of these counter-narratives involves more than just documenting physical changes. It entails questioning the operational images, as described by Haroun Farocki—those images created for practical application within technical and political systems, which often obscure the reality on the ground. The blueprints, satellite images, and official visuals associated with the Ilisu Dam are examples of such images that serve the project's functionality while masking its broader impacts. By juxtaposing these with participatory and community-driven representations, the project exposes the underlying extractivist motives and the enduring marks left on the landscape.

Neoliberal visual regimes have long emphasized narratives of modernization and economic gain while marginalizing the social and ecological repercussions. The Ilisu Dam's promotion utilized such a regime, presenting it as an emblem of advancement while neglecting the voices of those it dispossessed. Deconstructing these visual narratives reveals the deeper realities of displacement and cultural erasure. This project aims to craft a decolonial visual language that acknowledges and preserves the complexities of the land, people, and heritage.

Fieldwork in the region included gathering oral histories, photographic evidence, and video documentation to create a holistic, multi-sensory archive. Such an approach underscores the importance of community participation in reconstructing history and geography to reflect their lived experiences. The submerged roads, villages, and ancient structures documented through these efforts symbolize not just loss but resistance rooted in memory and hope for the future.

"Resisting Against Ecocide: Remapping Hasankeyf and Tigris" argues for reconsidering how development and progress are represented. It calls for visual and mapping practices that center the perspectives of those affected, emphasizing the need for ecological sustainability and cultural preservation. Through alternative mapping, the project reveals that the stories of resilience, continuity, and resistance remain vivid and crucial for shaping the future, even in the face of ecocide.
