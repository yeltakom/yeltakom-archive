Anatomy of a Night: Spaces Reborn in the Dark

Archive no.
2305
Published in
Manifold
Date
2023–2024
Original
Bir Gecenin Anatomisi: Karanlıkta Yeniden Doğan Mekânlar, Turkish
manifold.press ↗

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

When the lights on a dance floor are too bright, everyone's self-awareness rises, everyone feels weird and nobody wants to dance. Only when the lights are dim can we create that unifying space where we feel we are moving as a single collective organism, which in some respects is the ultimate aim of every dance floor's love.1

The darkness of the night hides endless possibilities: it contains within it a revolt against the work, order or routine that the day dictates. While raves, which usually begin in the dark, connect to the next day with the first lights of day, they carry a great hope that we can carry what the night promises into the day as well. With the anonymity and uncertainty that darkness provides, the night offers the potential for boundaries to stretch and norms to dissolve, for a freer way of being and being together, while raves make us feel that we can carry this potential into our everyday lives too. Far beyond partying as if there were no tomorrow, the dance floor plays an important role in making the day more inclusive, safe and free. This text tries to look at the relationship of rave with space, and at the social history of these spaces, through Berghain in Berlin and fabric in London, two of the world's leading clubs.2

There are many bloody and violent nights in world history, but the night of 9 November 1989 can be counted as an exception. Toward midnight the East German government had begun to lift the barricades and crossing controls, starting from the Brandenburg Gate, and people from both sides had begun to come together and meet on top of the wall. This meeting rebuilt, reconnected not only the two divided societies of one country, but dozens of different combinations. The areas that were in East Berlin but were left in the very middle of the city, with their ruins and idle buildings, had become the centre of a new movement. The unused industrial areas of the city, open to discovery, had begun to become very suitable venues for party culture. Tresor,3 one of the pioneering venues of this combination, founded in 1991 by Dimitri Hegemann, brought together young people from East and West Berlin after the fall of the wall, while also strengthening cultural connections by inviting names from the Detroit scene. Although techno is thought to have been born in Detroit, even Juan Atkins, one of Detroit's pioneers, says he was inspired by the German musicians Kraftwerk. With the fall of the wall, techno can in a sense be said to have returned to where its seeds were sown, and the futuristic and minimal structure of this music can be read as a hope that grew with reunification.

Berghain, Google Earth image

The story of Berghain, founded in 2004, begins with Norbert Thormann and Michael Teufele organising Snax, a men-only party that is still considered one of Europe's largest fetish parties, in the Bunker, now used by the Boros Collection in Berlin Mitte. Afterwards they founded their first club, Ostgut, in an old railway repair depot in Friedrichshain, one of the popular places left over from East Berlin; unlike Snax, this place appealed to wider crowds, and in a sense it can be called the beginning of the road to Berghain.

When you get off at Ostbahnhof and start walking toward Berghain, you encounter a huge neoclassical facade. The queue in front of this former thermal power plant building, built in 1953 as part of the postwar Stalinallee development and abandoned in the 1980s, draws attention as a long line of people in thrilled anticipation. Once inside, you meet a monumental brutalist architecture with 18-metre ceilings and wide interiors. It can be thought that Berghain's position in the middle of the city is reflected in its name too: before the wall fell, on one side of the building was Kreuzberg from West Berlin and on the other side Friedrichshain from East Berlin; Berghain is a play on words born from the names of these two neighbourhoods.

One of the most important devices of the mysterious structure that makes Berghain so famous is its strict door policy. The black-focused dress code is thought to be one of the factors that determine entry, but at the same time everyone inside dresses in the way they can express themselves most comfortably. Beyond following the dress rules or displaying a certain attitude to get into the venue, seeking suitability for the atmosphere inside aims to protect the experience within the club. This practice is thought to be discriminatory, but at the same time it is, sadly, this very practice that creates the regular-visitor culture and community inside, which can sometimes turn into an advantage for certain groups. The club's rigid and unclear door policy mostly gives rise to speculation. The time spent waiting in line to get in can sometimes reach four hours. Since no advance tickets are sold, when you reach the metal barriers that begin about thirty metres from the entrance, the bouncer's judgment process at the door begins. The person facing the bouncer may be refused entry without any reason being given, or be sent off to buy a ticket with a "Viel Spass" (have fun). Sven Marquardt,4 one of the club's famous bouncers, is a name known by regulars and in the techno scene. A detailed body search is done at the entrance, and stickers are put on phone cameras.

Perhaps one of the features that set Berghain apart from its counterparts in the world is that it follows a sex-positive policy. According to Johan Andersson's article "Berghain: Space, Affect, and Sexual Disorientation",5 Berghain can be considered not only a nightclub but also a space where spatial and emotional boundaries are crossed and sexuality is redefined. With its architecture and sound system, Berghain creates a distinctive environment in which identities and interactions are transformed, which allows the community inside to become a more fluid and permeable collective.

While techno plays in the main space, house dominates in Panorama Bar upstairs. The fact that the dance floors and the resting areas in the venue are almost equal in size also affects the club experience positively. Perhaps in a few centuries Berghain will be remembered as a house of worship.

Sampo Hanninen, "Empiric Studies", 2011

In Panorama Bar the curtains open and I realise day is breaking. The dancers here restore my energy. After dancing with them for a while I set out to go to the espresso bar downstairs. Seeing my name written on one of the booths I pass, I am confused. As I reach for my phone to photograph it, I remember the sticker I put on my camera. Just before going down, I start watching the dance floor below. The crowd, which turns into a faint yellow blur in the dark, turns into different patterns as it moves. On the way home, walking along the canal watching those meeting for breakfast and those walking their dogs, I hear again a song that was playing inside. Mutlu says what I heard was a church bell. Perhaps he is right. ("Ne Mutlu, Gece Gibi…" (How Happy, Like the Night…), 2024)

Although it was not as dramatic a break as the fall of the Berlin Wall, the decline of industrial production from the 1950s, the spread of automation technologies and, along with them, an economic structure fed more by the service sector led to the emptying and idling of industrial areas in cities like London. Just as Mitte was left in the middle after the fall of the Berlin Wall, Farringdon too became an ideal place for London, with both its central location and its abandoned industrial buildings, for creative projects and nightclubs.

fabric, Google Earth image

fabric, opened in London's Farringdon in 1999, has been named the best club in the world several times by DJ Magazine. The club, which closed in 2016 after two deaths, reopened with increased security measures and restrictions following social media and crowdfunding campaigns launched for its reopening. These measures include a photo ID checking system at the entrance that can be read as discriminatory, a two-stage detailed bag and body search, and club staff who walk around inside constantly with red-lit torches and do not allow any place to stay in the dark. As in many clubs, photography is forbidden here too; thanks to the stickers put on the front and back cameras of phones at the entrance, participants unfamiliar with club culture also comply with this rule.

Finding the venue, converted from a meat storage depot called Metropolitan Cold Stores, reportedly took about ten years. The history of the area goes back to the 19th century. The wide stairs connecting the three different dance floors inside the venue appear as the most prominent element. Of the three spaces named by number, "Room 1" has a special floor. Thanks to the floor fitted with 400 bass transducers, the bass can be felt in the body, and this experience is called "bodysonic". The resident DJs Craig Richards and Terry Francis have played almost every Saturday since the club opened. In the venue, dominated by house and techno, besides the dance floors there are resting areas made up of armchairs and various seating units, and an outdoor area where smoking is allowed.

The club's sterility begins to be felt the moment you go through the face scan to get in. Unlike Berghain, fabric has no pronounced door policy. Anyone with a ticket is let in, as long as they make no extreme move. Although this is a much more inclusive and democratic approach, it causes fabric to fall short on the "community" factor, which stands out most when evaluating a rave. It is hard to say there is any sense of community inside, and the safe space that is created, sadly, does not form organically. The perception of a safe space here does not go beyond constantly experiencing the presence of the rather numerous "awareness team" walking around with red torches in their hands.

Spaces that do not provide enough darkness, wide stairs and the parts that open onto outdoor areas also prevent the experience of breaking away from daily life. This provides an environment in which awareness inside is high. In fact, it is quite hard to provide the utopia that rave can offer as this kind of institution, because what must be done to create the environment that feeds the idea of this utopia can conflict with the precautions that must be taken to guarantee the health of the crowd inside. That fabric, which offers a much more normative experience than Berlin clubs, was used as a Berlin club in the series Killing Eve is an ironic detail about the club.
