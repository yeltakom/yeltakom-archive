The Architect's Confusion in a Participatory Exhibition Project

Archive no.
1713
Published in
Manifold
Date
14 August 2017
Original
Katılımcı Sergi Projesinde Mimarın Kafa Karışıklığı, Turkish
manifold.press ↗

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

When I read [Yelta's account, in last week's [30/07/2017] Berlin Notları, of his experiences of the participatory exhibition project I curated](https://manifold.press/berlin-notlari-8), I was not surprised. Because I had noticed how confused he had become during the process, in a short evaluation we did while preparing the press kit. So when he said he would write about the exhibition, I began waiting with curiosity. Although I was beside him while he finished the piece, I deliberately did not want to read it before publication, so as not to influence it in any way. When on Sunday morning I read the self-criticism "I was making all the decisions, and I was fully aware that these came more from the head of a single man than from a participatory process", I thought I needed to add a few words of my own. Because that is not how it went.

I should state at the outset that the aim of this text is certainly not to refute what Yelta wrote. My aim is to take this confusion, through the participatory project curating practice that I apply, as an opportunity for a discussion based on examples in the context of architecture, exhibition design and participation. For that, we have to go back to the very beginning, to the first conversation Yelta and I had. To that moment when we realised that we had both a great deal in hand and, within such dense material, in fact nothing concrete.

Leaving the meeting with the exhibition venue, I sent Yelta a message. "I've sent you the plan of the space, let's talk when you have time." He replied. "OK, what's the budget?" It was our most basic worry. "There's no budget, we'll make something with what we have." After I listed over the phone what we had, there was a silence on Yelta's end. "Çiğdem, there's a very high chance this place will turn into a bazaar (kermes) area." It was the women's and our biggest concern. "Well, not doing that is exactly your architectural skill."

To be honest, the moment the Göçmen Kadınlar Birliği (GKB, Association of Migrant Women) received the exhibition proposal, the women were gripped by the fear of the unknown. They had organised dozens of events before, run campaigns, set up small exhibition areas at festivals, but preparing an exhibition for IG Metall-Haus Berlin was a field they did not know and in fact could hardly imagine. Before that, we had done another exhibition project, again with Yelta involved: Göçmen Kadınlar Birliği'nin ABC'si: Dişil Bir Sözlük (The ABC of the Association of Migrant Women: A Feminine Dictionary). GKB, whose headquarters is in Frankfurt, carries out organised work in more than twenty cities through local associations and commissions. For this reason, a call to take part in the exhibition project was made from the central management to the regions. In this project, which involved more than two hundred women in eleven cities and was quite hard to run, we had determined the exhibition design from the start. Because depending on the concept we had certain priorities. It was going to be a pop-up exhibition, and therefore the materials had to be portable. For this reason Yelta proposed printing on canvas. We approved this idea only with the exhibition advisory team. But of course the women in the regions also stated their ideas and/or objections about canvas printing in their conversations with me. I resolved these without passing them on to Yelta at all. So in that exhibition Yelta was able to carry out the architect's role within the project hierarchy without bending it at all. I cannot say the same for Elif (Çak), who arranged the visuals, for example. Elif first had to work out how to adapt the visuals coming from the women into a common graphic language. Some visuals were very complicated, and some were incompatible. Again I would talk to the regions and pass on to Elif what they had in mind, and I would send the drafts Elif prepared to the regions and ask for their approval. I was a kind of negotiator. One day when we were going through the exhibition booklet, Elif said, "I prepared all the visuals for print, but I am still very much a stranger to some of them." Because her role as graphic designer had dissolved in the hierarchy. Some participants really liked the graphics, and some asked why the visual they had sent had been turned into line drawings. Neither Elif nor Yelta took part in these discussions. Or rather, could not. We had to prepare the exhibition in a short time, ten, fifteen days, and so we had not been able to create an opportunity to bring the women and the project team together. In the workshops I was always the one who relayed. I relayed the architecture and graphic design to the women, and passed the women's comments on to the design team, creating a common path. But I was aware that this was incomplete representation. That is why in the Dişil Perspektiften Dünya! (The World from a Feminine Perspective!) exhibition I took care to bring the parties together.

We had received the plan of the exhibition venue from IG Metall, but this document, full of endless numbers, meant nothing to the women. So it was very important for us that Yelta drew the space. The space divided into rooms, and seeing from different angles how the dictionary, the magazine covers, the photographs, the tables and the like, decided on as exhibition materials, sat in the hall, opened up ground for us to discuss the exhibition materials. We met Mehtap (Çallı) and Pelin (Şener), who were running the coordination of the project on behalf of GKB. In the meeting Yelta noted all the suggestions coming from the women, explaining what could not be done and why. Or he developed a suggestion with other materials. For example, in that meeting we discussed at length how the photo album would be displayed. Yelta explained what kind of material we needed, and talked about the depth that transparency in the material would add to the space. Giving that knowledge of depth made the women approach the material suggestions differently. They suggested, for example, pinning things onto tulle curtains. On the preliminary materials shopping round, if Yelta had not seen the lattices (or if the women had not accepted that idea), we would have gone to a side street and looked at tulle curtains.

Dişil Perspektiften Dünya!, installation, IG Metall-Haus Berlin, 2017, photographs: Pelin Şener

The most direct example in this project of architectural practice not being applicable within a hierarchical structure may be the tables, which Yelta also mentions in his text. In the drawing Yelta had specified the tables as purple and orange, and had given the colour codes. But during shopping it became clear that we could not paint the tables. Because they needed sanding. And for that we had neither the people nor the time. Eyes turned to me. I called Yelta and explained the situation. First he tried to convince me how beautiful they would be if they were painted. I said this was no longer a possibility. "Then let the legs be white." The painting job was not as easy as expected. Those table legs dragged on for a week. But since the women did not want the legs to stay green and give a Biergarten feel either, the job was finally completed in a quick and organised operation. In the meantime there were also decisions about the table that we made with the women without consulting Yelta at all. The legs of one table would not open. Replacing it with a new one was an option, but when we decided that the materials we had were fewer than we had anticipated, we took that table out. The section that had been set at three tables in the space design thus went down to two. During the installation, when the placement of the tables could not be decided, Yelta stepped in again. Together with the women they dragged the tables this way and that and placed them. Looking at the decisions that were carried out, Yelta may think the whole process was determined by his own ideas, but in the participatory project curating practice that I persistently try to follow, his task was to produce new alternatives until the women were convinced. As curator, I was doing the same.

What matters for my responsibility is process management rather than the end product. It must absolutely be based on equal representation. Therefore there is no hierarchical relationship structure between the women and the "professional team" involved in the project. Since it is not possible for the parties to come together continuously, I take on the task of facilitation as curator. I carry back and forth between the women and the design team the changes/suggestions made during the process on the design agreed on in its basic principles. My aim here is to be able to translate between two different languages. Because the women have to work out the technical language of the design team, and the design team has to work out the feasibility of the ideas the women produce from practice. In this latest exhibition project too, I was the curator of the project, but I had only a single role: "Keeping the train on the track." People were constantly getting off and on, and everyone was making a suggestion about the direction we should go. Everything could change at any moment, a decision taken could be abandoned within a few hours. I kept a record of all this, both informing the relevant parties and putting the tasks in order of priority and following up on their completion. In such a structure, among dozens of examples, I can also describe the discussion about placing a female figure in the space, to show the limits of the architect's intervention.

The venue manager had told us at the first meeting that we should design "something eye-catching" for the shop window of the space that looks directly onto the building's entrance. Yelta placed colourful boxes in this area and the basis of the "Sandık" (Chest) section was formed. The idea of placing here a female figure, which they had used in their earlier campaigns, was also discussed in the meeting with Mehtap and Pelin. Yelta also proposed materials suited to the concept of the space. But none of these could be carried out for various reasons. A search for other materials began. Yelta absolutely opposed these new material proposals and was worried that the whole natural feel of the space would be spoiled. So was I, in fact. We discussed this very often between us. At one point Yelta pulled out his architect card on me: "You'll say, 'Yelta said so'." I objected. The women had to decide for themselves. So we talked at length again with the women. In these meetings, which Yelta could not attend, since the material and the production did not sit right with anyone, it was given up. And nobody was left with the thought "we couldn't put in the female figure." Because by now we all knew why we couldn't.

Dişil Perspektiften Dünya!, installation, IG Metall-Haus Berlin, 2017, photographs: Pelin Şener

It was when we prepared the press kit that Yelta and I first talked about the matter of participation. He was then questioning that only his name appeared for the exhibition space design. The women had developed ideas too. "Yes, that's why every time we say it is a participatory exhibition project. Because the women were part of the process. For example, Pelin did the photo album section on her own, so we wrote her name there. And since the space design was carried out under your lead, we are writing your name. If you have another suggestion, of course tell me." A few days later he said he had not been able to come up with an alternative title for himself. That is when I began to think: what, in fact, distinguished a participatory project from group work? It does not matter how many people there were; in the end there was still a certain number of women who put labour into the exhibition. So at the end of the day we became a group. A participatory project exhibition group. Perhaps we were larger in number than usual group work. Perhaps some of us never saw each other. Some never even came to Berlin. Still, they stated their opinion, sent materials for the exhibition from their region. In some way they took part in the process and therefore in the exhibition.

Whether the absolute distribution of roles in this design process has to do with architecture, I do not know either. Because as far as I observed, when Arda (Bakıryol) came for the installation, he too hesitated from time to time over making decisions. He was living the dilemma of staying faithful to the drawings versus producing a solution suited to the situation. The most striking example may be the welcome board. In Yelta's drawings the board stood next to the magazine covers. When it was decided to hang more magazine covers, there was no room left on the wall for the board. At that moment Arda saw the empty wall on the other side of the door: "Shall we hang it here?" Asking Yelta became a brief item on our agenda between us, but we did not do it. When Yelta came that evening to see how the installation had turned out, he saw this. He was also pleased that Arda had intervened in the space together with the women. Because the women had also completed the arrangement of the "Sandık" section. For instance, wooden boxes had been bought in place of the colourful boxes in Yelta's drawing. The women decided together and did everything, from how they would be placed to how the materials would sit inside the boxes. And Yelta never even saw those women. When he saw the arrangement, he suggested replacing the banner made of tarpaulin with patchwork. His aim again was to preserve the natural concept of the space. Can this be called one man making all the decisions?

In this frame, I think that what confused Yelta in this project (to use his example) has to do with making room for who will intervene, how much, and how. It could also be expressed as going back and forth between leaving the architect's 'creative' identity behind and saying "here you are, use my drawings in the way that suits you best". Because in my view, in a participatory exhibition project the architect should both protect their own spatial concept and enable the participants to intervene in the space, within this determined design, in ways through which they can express themselves. Even if in the intense process Yelta's head was confused about how far he could stretch these areas, in the work that finally emerged, as can also be seen in the examples I have just mentioned, Yelta did not make the decisions. This was the most fundamental difference separating this project from the first dictionary exhibition we did. Certainly, once again it was not complete. We could have ensured more participation from the women. We could have had more meetings between the parties. Yelta could have held a workshop with the women for the space design. After the opening we evaluated the exhibition process with the women from these angles. We talked about what we could develop, and how, in the next project. In this context I see Yelta's text, and the words I add here in reply to it, as part of this discussion. All these discussions are in any case a natural part of participatory project practice. And so are all the confusions that lead us to discussion.

Yelta Köm
Berlin
Represented by Versus Art Project, Istanbul
