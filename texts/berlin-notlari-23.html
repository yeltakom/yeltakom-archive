Berlin Notes #23

Archive no.
2103
Published in
Manifold
Date
29 April 2021
Original
Berlin Notları #23, Turkish
manifold.press ↗

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

Spring could not come to Berlin again. It is almost May but winter coats are still out, and if there is no sun the cold shows itself. The nicest side of this city's spring that never arrives is that it reminds you of the summer sun that does not set. Last month I was in Istanbul. I had been longing for it for ages. After staying away this long for the first time, I stayed a long while. On the day the World Health Organization declared the pandemic I had only passed through the airport on a connecting flight, and everything after that was digital meetings. How much I had missed the Boğaz, Istanbul, every corner of it, neighborhood life...

Moda, Istanbul

On the way back to Berlin I had inside me both the joy of returning and feelings like sadness, like anger. This state of being split in two had taken firm hold after staying so long. For years I had not traveled for such a long time, nor stayed in the same place. I think staying fixed in one place increased the feeling of duality. Writing used to do me good, actually, but in the pandemic it was very hard for me; instead of writing it was easier to record things visually. But writing differs from taking photographs in recording: the words pour out after passing through dozens of invisible stages that you go over again and again. And isn't it also one of the acts done not only for oneself but for the future, in order not to die? On the other hand, isn't writing notes belonging to a place you have gone to, and sharing them, really saying "One day I will come" to those left behind?

All the way back I read Demir Özlü's Berlin Güncesi (Berlin Diary), and listening to him about the Berlin of years ago, I again said to myself "How fixed everything is." And it tells of the time just before the German Democratic Republic was erased from the stage of history; diaries with West Berlin at the center. He talks about the weather a lot too, this city's business with the weather truly never ends. The international culture and art scene, those who leave and those who arrive, the parties, the invitations, are so similar. Özlü also tells homesickness stories like today's: he mentions that Güney Dal took him into a börek (savory pastry) shop in Kreuzberg and that the shop owner was an architect. He thinks that this architect, who also makes tel kadayıf and baklava (shredded-dough and layered-filo desserts), makes them out of homesickness. Today too, dozens of people cut off from their homelands do other jobs in this city, without practicing their own professions or without wanting to. You might come across similar stories at record dealers, at market stalls.

Demir Özlü, Berlin Güncesi, page 52

Berlin has become a place not only for those who fall into new stories, but where hundreds of people who want to change their lives and conditions have come and keep coming in recent years. Although the dynamic within the city has not been very visible in the last year, online communities are working quite actively. This internationality sometimes gives one the feeling of living inside a closed bubble. We come together in our own fields, in the same urban or virtual spaces as people like us. But I still will not romanticize it as an understanding place where all who are unlike each other can breathe. Yes, it is a fact that it is a city with higher tolerance, but it is not a utopia either. At heart, not everyone enjoys Berlin's poor but sexy state. Yet what excites me most about this city is that it equalizes people, that socializing is almost classless, the culture of solidarity spread across the grassroots. I am aware that protecting these will not be easy, it is not an anti-capitalist dream either, but there is something that works.

As you know, I have been talking for a long time about housing needs in Berlin and tenant movements. Most of the city are tenants. The regulation that capped rents and banned increases was annulled. People took to the streets again to protest this decision, and signatures are being collected again right now. People's rents, which had fallen, have risen again, and they are expected to pay back the difference that caused the drop in recent months. Isn't shelter, the right to housing, among our primary rights? Isn't it a human right? I think owning property distances people from such thoughts, and the landlords are generally big firms. I do not know whether it is enough to say "It is gentrifying" for Berlin today, but my faith that this city will stand against all the neoliberal policies that come is complete. Because of my interest in this subject I also follow online real estate groups. In these groups, where the talk is mostly in English, the people in them often ask one another "how to buy a home in Berlin". Frankly, I both learn how the market works and sometimes have quite a bit of fun reading the comments. Those who say that compared to the building quality in America, Germany is far better, those who shake off bureaucracy, those who write comments like "I want to buy a home in Berlin but I don't plan to come here for ten years, I'll do Airbnb"... In this group I followed silently, the rent regulation was also discussed from time to time, and a part of the group would say things like "Drop these old-fashioned ideas, free market." On the day the regulation was annulled, someone in the group posted, in a rough translation, something like this: "We must celebrate this! Goodbye regulation!" It is a fact that this regulation made landlords happy. But do we close our eyes this much to the rest, to the people in the city who will breathe easier with this regulation? At the end of the day, those who legitimize themselves within the free-market nonsense can also argue that this is their individual right. My leaving the group happened with this post. Without even joining the heated debates under it, I just reported the post in question to the group's admins as "hate content". What do you think happened? I was kicked out of the group. For the first time I felt excluded in this city, and it happened to be with an expat group.

I feel as if I am saving what I set aside from what I read, watch and listen to for an exhibition that will never happen. Perhaps, like Burak Çevik's "Yapmayacağım Filmler" (Films I Will Not Make), it would do me good to write the exhibitions I will not make, the buildings I will not make; or the images that keep appearing and vanishing in my mind from the film I want to make will surely fall onto a screen one by one someday.

Yelta Köm
Berlin
