Berlin Notes #38: Dancing with the Breath, Sunny but Cold

30 September 2024

Berlin Notları #38: Nefesle Dans, Güneşli Ama Soğuk, Turkish

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

“‘Behind my thoughts there is no other city that I feel at home in every time I arrive.’ ‘Do you tell your therapist why you don't stay at home?’ I could not answer, I could not even say that I had not talked to him for a long time. Like the placelessness of the therapy I do through a screen, the home too has now lost its walls. If there are homes and selves that find a response in my heart, then we are, and if there are not, if we are not, we are not.” Tristan da Cunha'da Bir Sabah (A Morning in Tristan da Cunha), p. 26

In the last days of September I love Berlin more. It is as if I am living that familiar rush that came with the opening of school in my childhood. The city slowly turns grey, cold but inviting places pull one in. Everyone has returned from their summer holiday, waiting to take shelter in one another. The city's cold, bluish daylight changes not only my photographs but all of my perception.

In the pale light of morning, I hear the cold rising beside the window. I have grown used to these changing tones of the air. In winter the daylight strikes from lower down, and perhaps this narrow angle hardens the air as well. These short days change the mood and rhythm of the city, turning it into the capital of the night. The relationships the body establishes with the air and with time change.

“My body is both seeing and visible. That which looks at everything can also look at itself, and in so doing recognise the ‘other side’ of its power of seeing. It sees itself seeing; it touches itself touching; it is visible and sensible for itself.” —Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Göz ve Tin (Eye and Mind), p. 33

Walking in a city is like dancing with that city's breath. What Merleau-Ponty wrote reminds me of this. Like Istanbul's warm and yellow evenings, within Berlin's heavy slowness there is room for hedonist feelings.

I had met my friend Beppe fifteen years ago in Beirut, and those were the days I remember fondly of the beautiful country and city that is under bombs today. He had just finished a research trip in Syria and, after staying a few months in Milan, had come to Beirut again. The year was 2010 and most of what we know today had not yet begun; he was working on a photo series that took up "things that do not exist or will no longer exist".

Beirut has taken a place within me, in my heart, ever since that journey. With my Sicilian friend, over the past years, we have met in different countries and cities. I do not know whether it is because my astrological chart perhaps passes through Italy, but this Italian world has always made me feel like a second home. We last met in Brussels, and while talking about Berlin I called it a "hedonist" city. I had not thought much about this phrase that came out of my spinal cord, but then I did; sometimes both falling and rising are hedonist acts, with seeing and being seen inside them.

Berlin's hedonist nature recalls a deep melancholy on one side and exuberance on the other.

However many years I have been saying that this city was not made for idling, when this inward-turned idling state of mine intersects with daylight, I constantly discover other tones and surfaces. The city's wrapping itself toward darkness makes me lose myself in a cycle both physical and temporal. This cycle creates spatial and temporal reflections; while getting lost in the city, I find myself.

There is someone I am surprised I never mentioned in my notes until today: Gerhard Richter. During my master's in Frankfurt, after visiting his solo exhibition at the Fondation Beyeler in Basel and seeing his paintings with my own eyes, my way of looking at painting changed and my questions about the matter of representation came back to life. Richter's blurred paintings, in which he tries to show that reality cannot be represented absolutely, seem to me a good method for the relationship we establish with the city. This blur shakes the reliability of memory and visual perception while disturbing both the seer and the seen. On the one hand it has a critical stance toward memories, history and visual media. On the other it tries to represent our uncertainties and emotional movements in the face of historical traumas.

Gerhard Richter, “Stadtbild SA (219/1)”, 1969, source: Google Arts & Culture

Richter painted “Stadtbild SA” in 1969, in the times when the intense division created by the Berlin Wall was felt. This painting treats the city both abstractly and concretely. One could say "this is a bit like idling in the city". The scarcity of colours reminds me of the short Berlin winter, while it evokes the slowness of time flowing inside the houses. We spend more time at home, indoors.

This painting leaves an uneasy feeling on me, almost like a ghost, just like the house. Perhaps it is Richter's paintings and their blur that change both belonging and our present day. Houses and belonging are blurred and uncertain like these paintings, standing without being fixed. While thinking about today's Berlin, now look at Richter's paintings and listen to “Nova Raks”. This piece by Hassan Abou El Seoud, one of Egypt's most famous composers and accordionists, familiar to us from oriental music, opens for me a melancholic and equally uncertain path for all this blur and for the complexity of the state of the world we are in. Raks means dance, nova means new, and in this blurred world, with the breath of the city, why not this new dance?

“The mornings of the last days of this summer I will not forget melt in the light of my eyes. Today is Monday, the museums are asleep and the city seems livelier, playing its own turn. I try to go to work, but I to you, you to me, in neighbouring houses, a sunny November morning warms the façade.” O Yaz (That Summer), p. 43
