Berlin Notes #34: Radiye

Archive no.
2304
Published in
Manifold
Date
30 September 2023
Original
Berlin Notları #34: Radiye, Turkish
manifold.press ↗

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

Recently, migration to Germany from every segment of society has begun to increase. This has become part of our everyday conversations too. Sometimes everyone is searching for a way forward and people help one another. One of the main reasons I love Berlin is that it accepts everyone as they are, and that I feel this deeply. From the migrant neighbourhoods looked down on by some to its popular corners, the feeling of freedom the city offers is above everything.

Along with skilled labour, I have started meeting idealistic engineers more often. I mostly think that these new migrants coming to Berlin, not only from Turkey but from different parts of the world, see themselves as outside the migrant status because they call themselves "expats". In many forums and Facebook groups I have entered out of personal curiosity, they claim that Berlin is disorderly, that some neighbourhoods cannot be entered, that crime rates rise where "Middle Easterners" are. I attribute this condescending approach to the acceptance of "Europeanness". There are places in this geography that are as they want them to be. In the groups and forums I am talking about, jokes such as "you can go to Munich or Frankfurt" are generally made in response to these comments.

The experiences of recent Turkish migrants in Berlin are not the same as those of the 60s. In any case, no migration story is identical to another. Each is unique and layered. Over the years the Turkish-origin community in Berlin has changed. From labour migrants to family reunification and now to a new wave of educated migrants, the Turkish diaspora in Berlin forms a rich mosaic of varied experiences. The new wave of recent years is known for its higher educational qualifications and its focus on the IT sector. But unfortunately, on platforms like Twitter and Facebook we also encounter approaches that mercilessly criticise situations that do not match their former comfortable lives (yes, Berlin is not a clean city, nor does it offer gated-community life) or that directly target other migrants. Sadly, at times there are even words and behaviours that run parallel to the discourse of the rising right wing in Germany. It is sad to see that such an approach will one day reach the point of attributing every criminal incident to migrants, of speculating about what happens in certain neighbourhoods without any concrete evidence. In past years, a self-confident newly arrived friend stated that the Turkish-origin community in Germany was uneducated and isolated from German society, that the newly arriving educated segment could build a bridge between these two groups, and that he wanted to do something about it. It is truly sad that he presented this sincere but ignorantly bold approach, and moreover believed in it, on the basis of two days of observation. They do not know that the Turkish-origin people in Germany have already been active for years and are within society. What makes Germany and Berlin special is this richness and complexity, this plurality of voices. Perhaps this idea of "arranging" comes from the nature of an engineering education, but it does not fit the dynamic structure of Berlin. This subject affects me because of both my personal story and the people I know. It brings Radiye to my mind.

Radiye, Yelta Köm archive

Some names move against the fate assigned to them. The dictionary meaning of Radiye is "one who consents, who submits". But Radiye, my paternal grandmother, who appears almost as a main character in the Berlin Notes series, has an important role in shaping my life today. Her mother was born in Romania, her father in Macedonia. Nearly every member of her family had put down roots in different lands, but in the end they all came together in Üsküdar.

Within the guest worker programme that Germany launched in the post-war period to fill its labour shortage, first my great-uncle and then my grandfather joined this road. Radiye's joining it was on her own initiative. At that time she had two children in Istanbul, and telling the story of her own arrival, she says, "I came by car, I convinced everyone." I know that when she first arrived in Berlin she and my grandfather stayed in separate hostels, but I do not know much about what she went through afterwards. I know that her home in Charlottenburg, where she still lives today, at first had a shared bathroom, and that they later had a bathroom built inside the apartment. She tells of bringing chocolate in a suitcase when she was working at a chocolate factory, and later working at a sock factory and at a light bulb company. After that she took on part-time jobs. I still have in mind to sit down one day and record her whole story, but for now this portrait, put together from half-finished stories heard second hand, is enough for me.

Radiye's children and grandchildren live in Germany and in Turkey. All of them work and carry on with their lives. The most important thing I feel in the extended family is tolerance. I think part of this tolerance comes from the chemistry of the city and the society. I find this intermittent way of living very rich. Later I think of a Syrian family I met in Istanbul. This family sets an example for the new wave of migration that refers to every criminal incident and increases discrimination, for those who, the moment something bad happens, say without thinking "The cause is Middle Easterners."

In 2015, after finishing my master's degree, I was in Istanbul for a while. I received an e-mail from my friend Andreas, whom I knew from Germany. A Syrian family fleeing the war, a mother and three children, would land at Sabiha Gökçen Airport in two days. Their father was in Berlin. Could I help them? I was very new to this field at the time, but I called everyone I knew and asked for help. Finding a place to stay was not easy. I took the mother and her three children and found a short-term place around Taksim. Later we ate together. In this unfinished story, the mother is in fact a mathematics teacher and the father in Berlin is a lawyer. After a while, with the help of acquaintances, we found a house. Together with Elif we settled them in and did what we could. We too became part of the story. But what I saw throughout the process was that this family neither submitted nor consented. Years later they were reunited with their father and they are now in Berlin. In the research projects on migration I have worked on over the years, I have listened to the different paths of migration, the painful experiences, the distortion of the perception of time, all the hardships that were lived.

I cannot see myself within the new wave of migration. Perhaps it is because of these constant comings and goings. I moved to Berlin after Frankfurt. I have old friends, but slowly I have started to see the newcomers too. I meet many highly paid people working in the technology and software sectors. A feeling inside me says that these people project the unhappiness from the places they come from onto Berlin. This is quite natural. But a group of highly educated migrants claim that it is not possible to live in Berlin without being a leftist and loving techno music. I think there is room in Berlin for everyone. But outside Berlin, in the other cities of Germany, I too feel tension. At least in this city, living without being judged is everyone's right. Berlin may not be the Europe of our dreams, but what makes this city special is not only its buildings but also its people. At the same time, perhaps all my being triggered is simply the contempt shown for the stories of these people who came without consenting, without submitting.
