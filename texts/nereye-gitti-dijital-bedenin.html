Nereye Gitti Dijital Bedenin?

Archive no.
1806
Published in
Manifold
Date
28 January 2018
Original
Nereye Gitti Dijital Bedenin?, Turkish
manifold.press ↗

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

While drawing, one can sometimes drift into long daydreams. I find myself thinking, unlike with hand drawing, about the people we use when we draw on the computer, build models, even prepare images.

Inside a three-dimensional model we copy and copy and place two- and three-dimensional human figures. Whose body am I reproducing? Depending on the copying method, sometimes they are cloned one to one, and sometimes they become independent pieces of data.

Let me introduce you to Chris, for example. Chris greets us on the opening screen of the quite popular modeling application SketchUp. Hands in his pockets, fully self-confident; as I wonder who he is, the program's interface says that he is the company's sales engineering lead. It is possible to copy Chris, to clone him. It is possible to create an army of him or to bring him back to life in other environments. But even if digital data keeps hiding its uniqueness, what does Chris do when we close the file?

It sounds like science fiction, doesn't it? Or like a story from "Toy Story": the life of human figures wandering through data. With a small search on the internet you can find dozens of these standard human figures. This is one of those magnificent moments when our obsession with originality and uniqueness is exposed, because although they all look the same, they are different: the way they were produced digitally at that moment, their locations, even the physical places they occupy in memory differ from one another.

Human scale, an important complement of architectural representation, makes a drawing, an image or a model of unspecified scale suddenly relatable. Some projects have friends who are constantly on the move, for example; over time they slip from place to place between e-mails and find themselves a spot in everybody's project. The files they are in usually have names like sablon.dwg (template.dwg), insan.dwg (human.dwg), derli-toplu.dwg (tidy.dwg).

At one point we got fixated on these ready-made figures and racked our brains to create our own. We tried it in a few projects, but that did not continue either. Like the people I use in collages, they too had become standardized. It often feels strange to me that we brush past this relationship between the human body and space on the computer screen as if it were very simple.

Today, when we all live with our electronic avatars in different ways and have turned into cyborgs, it honestly feels pleasant to think about our representation left alone inside the computer. Years ago I made portraits of these friends, and I suggest you look at these images for a long time, accompanied by the piece below. Anton Arensky's composition could also keep a Sunday company nicely.

I do not know who these people are. They are not like Chris either; where they come from is unknown. They just exist on their own, perhaps produced without any reference at all. Whose hand did they first come out of, how many of them are there, how long does this dark loneliness last?

I do not know.

If we leave our abstract friends aside and focus on those who travel the world through photographs, we can find more entertaining examples. The most famous source of human figures used especially for architectural collages is undoubtedly Skalgubbar, started by Teodor Javanaud Emdén. In these drawings, which feature relatively urban, hipster characters, the image you will see most is this one, the one with his back turned to us.

These mostly white, Scandinavian images spread very fast because of how easy they are to reach. They suddenly occupied every architectural imagination, yet was architecture really this far from diversity? Abstract human figures let us dream about who they could be and what they could do. But photographic images change, sometimes according to the place where the project will be built, sometimes according to the client; they become types that suit their sheath. It is the place where neighborhood pressure has been replaced by screen and render pressure, where debates about whether this character fits here keep circling.

In 2015, an article published on Archdaily listed five alternative websites to Skalgubbar. What set these sites apart was that they were render people databases that care about ethnic diversity. My favorite among them is Nonscandinavia. Not only the diversity but also what they write about the use of images and the silhouettes they create, in which faces cannot be made out, show their perspective on the subject. It proceeds as an open-source project.

In the coming pieces I plan to continue the conversation with these characters. I think digital avatars will take up more space in the future not only in architectural representation but in every area of our lives. Aren't even emoji a part of this today?
