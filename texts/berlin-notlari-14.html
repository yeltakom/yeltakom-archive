Berlin Notes #14

Archive no.
1908
Published in
Manifold
Date
30 November 2019
Original
Berlin Notları #14, Turkish
manifold.press ↗

AI translation from Turkish, not reviewed by the author

Lately Berlin has become very much like one of the cities of Türkiye. Famous YouTubers come and shoot videos here, music videos are set in Berlin. In every video Kreuzberg is told anew, and the indispensable part of every Berlin video is a chat, even if just a couple of words, with "the big brother of big brothers, the father Killa Hakan".*

So where does this Berlin love of recent years come from? The contribution of ever-increasing migration is undoubtedly great; in the earlier Berlin Notları I talked more or less about the newly arrived crowd. According to my personal observation, this crowd, mostly coming from information technologies and the creative industries, gathers in online groups and represents a new-generation migrant community. You may have heard it called the "new wave". It is true that the new wave has increased Berlin's popularity in Türkiye, but that is not the only reason. In recent years Berlin has been one of the most attractive not only of Türkiye's cities but of European cities too; a clear-eyed city that people from all over the world flock to every year, giving everyone a chance to exist.

People on the Berlin Wall, 9 November 1989, near the Brandenburg Gate. On the sign "Achtung! Sie verlassen jetzt West-Berlin" [Attention! You are now leaving West Berlin], "Wie denn?" [How?] has been written over the word "jetzt" (now), photograph: Sue Ream, source: Wikimedia Commons

The inspiring sides of this strange city, whose beauty is hidden in its ugliness, are quite few, but at the same time it insistently repeats situations that constantly renew a person's memory and especially do not tear the newcomer from where they came from. Perhaps because everyone who comes can find a tone of their own in this greyness, it creates an uneasy feeling of familiarity. Berlin is a city where different pasts come together; whether you come from Italy or from Argentina, you catch a tone of Berlin. Among the tones in the colour scale running from white to black, there are, at times, low walls and, at times, high ones. The matter of cultures coming together and becoming a mosaic often remains a layer thrown on to add colour to the picture. The lives, people and relationships that the wall, absent for thirty years, divided in two are no longer here today, but today Berlin has new walls; and ones built in ways that could not be predicted.

Migrant children collecting fragments of the Berlin Wall to sell to tourists, 1990, Kreuzberg, source: DiasporaTürk

Last week I went to a reading performance in Berlin. The performance Türkland, adapted from Dilşad Budak-Sarıoğlu's autobiographical work, made me think about the ways some of these new walls are built. The author, who left Türkiye by fleeing with her family, falls in love with it years later and returns, and on her wedding day enters a stream of thought about how she positions herself within the two cultures she feels part of and the traditions imposed on her. She conveys this stream of thought to the audience on stage, under the direction of İrem Aydın, together with Ilgıt Uçum. We watched the performance in Berlin with an audience mostly from Türkiye; from the content of the text it was clear that it was written for Türkiye and not for Germany. The text, which expressed very well the story of the "Almancı" (Germany-based migrant) that everyone in Türkiye knows, needled all the rote clichés in doing so, and said that a "migrant" is a migrant however many generations on. The fact that a migrant is a migrant is a reality often forgotten by newcomers, at times even reaching the point of seeing oneself as above. Although this educated, urban new crowd somehow thinks it belongs to another class of migrant, migration is a trauma that makes everyone equal. Although there are conveniences or privileges in some places, part of them are conveniences provided by the infrastructures built by those who came earlier, and by the troubles they went through.

Türkland, poster

In Türkland these troubles are laid out very clearly; along with them Solingen is mentioned, and Sivas too. All the tracks of the German education system are described, and the discrimination that migrant children are exposed to is discussed. One of the topics I saw as closest to myself was migrant illnesses; the title of the author's interview with *taz* is this: "We Are the Children of Generations Who Did Not See a Right to Exhaustion in Themselves." She speaks of the burnout syndrome that comes as a result of the struggle to stay in a place that is not one's own homeland; the result of traumas that cannot be confronted without realising. In the author's words, exhaustion is a situation not seen as a right for themselves after the hardships families went through; I, along with this, think that exhaustion is also a right not seen in order to be able to stay in this country, in order to be a good migrant. The hidden pressure of the effort to prove oneself in society makes a person merciless towards themselves, and when you have already started the match behind, things get really hard.

Home-made German-Turkish flag hung during the World Cup in Berlin-Neukölln in the summer of 2006, photograph: Rainer Zenz, source: Wikimedia Commons

The performance is realised as an Entropi Sahne production, in cooperation with Maviblau, the cultural platform between Berlin and Istanbul. It is staged in Turkish and German, with surtitles in these two languages. The performance dates vary, but it is possible to learn the next one from the social media accounts. It is a good opportunity to get to know more closely, and to question the stories of, Türkland, which most people living in Türkiye have at least one acquaintance in but have never fully visited. We know the wall that separated the two Berlins, we understand physically where they stood, whereas the walls surrounding Türkland are both changeable and invisible; it seems we still have time to understand them.

Berlin after the Second World War, Potsdamer Platz, photograph: Michael M. Dean (Canada. Dept. of National Defence/Ministère de la défense nationale), source: Wikimedia Commons

It has been exactly three years since I started the Berlin notes; after the feedback I have received and the breaks I have taken in that time, I have decided to talk about Berlin more often in Manifold. This city, which I have not understood for years but somehow have loved since my childhood, is perhaps so attractive because it holds answers to my question "where is home?". For a long time I have kept complaining that Berlin is not a city like a city, that you do not even feel like wandering aimlessly, that it has little surprise. In an urban fabric rebuilt almost entirely after the Second World War, of course it is very hard for me to find equivalents of the ready-made examples and urban situations in my head; perhaps that is why Berlin is like a very familiar stranger, and there is quite a lot to learn from it.

Berlin, photograph: Thomas Wolf, 2012, source: Wikimedia Commons

In 2012 the Governing Mayor of Berlin, Klaus Wowereit, called the city "poor but sexy", and afterwards, because of the airport that could not be finished, it became a laughing stock as "poor but incompetent". What has happened in recent years is that, as Berlin has become attractive, the gap between poverty and wealth has widened considerably. The crowding of the city by the creative sector and the information technology sector, with similar tastes and spending habits, is one of the factors opening this scissors. This cauldron will boil even more in the coming years; look, even Elon Musk has said he will build his new Tesla factory in Berlin. Everyone's interest, not only the Türkiye-based YouTubers', has shifted here. Perhaps, as Cohen said in his day, quite unexpectedly.

First we take Manhattan, then we take Berlin.

* Ben Fero's line from Killa Hakan's song "Fight Kulüp"; if you are wondering who Ben Fero is, let Özgün Çağlar's wonderful piece guide you.
